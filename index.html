The starting point

One of the most common uses of AI is also one of the most frustrating.

Making slides. Everyone tries it. Almost everyone is disappointed.

Hook: ask for a show of hands — "Who has asked an AI to make slides for them?" Then: "Keep your hand up if you were happy with the result." Hands drop. That gap — between how often we try and how rarely it works — is what this talk is about.
The tools all promise it

A crowded market of helpers

Specialised services

Gamma, Beautiful.ai — built entirely around generating decks from a prompt.

General assistants

Copilot, Claude, ChatGPT — all claiming they will sort out your PowerPoint for you.

The reality

You still spend your evening repairing what they produced.

Name the tools quickly — the audience will recognise them. The point isn't that the tools are bad; it's that we're asking them the wrong question. Tease: "I use presentations constantly — lectures, talks, training — and I only became happy with AI's help when I let go of one idea. We'll get to it."

The AI is fluent in the web. Then we force its work through a 40-year-old file format.

Key technical insight from the source material: when Manus, Claude, Gemini and friends "make slides", they first build an HTML page — their native medium — and then export it to PPTX. The export is where things break: fonts shift, boxes overflow, layouts collapse. Then you open it in PowerPoint, Slides or Canva and fix the barbarities one by one.
Why this workflow fails

Everyone loses

  • The AI loses the freedom to think differently — it must fit everything into rectangles PowerPoint understands.
  • You stay limited to what PowerPoint can do — which hasn't fundamentally changed since the 1990s.
×2

a double constraint

Spell out the double bind: the format constrains the AI's creativity AND caps your ambitions. Rhetorical question to the room: "What if we simply... skipped the export?"
The one idea to take home

Stop trying to make slides.
Start creating presentations.

A presentation is the act of presenting something — not the act of clicking "next" on rectangles.

THE thesis slide. Pause before and after the quote. A presentation should be conceived as presenting something to people — supporting a story, a lesson, a decision — not as flicking slides forward. Everything that follows is the practical consequence of this sentence.
The alternative

Build the presentation as a website

Keep the AI in its native medium: HTML. A "deck" becomes a small web experience that happens to advance like slides.

Site builders

Lovable, Google AI Studio — prompt-to-website services.

Coding agents

OpenAI Codex, Claude Code — desktop agents that build, test and refine the site for you.

This very talk

What you're watching now is one HTML file. No PowerPoint was harmed.

Reveal the meta-trick: THIS presentation is an HTML page — polls and all. Tools: you can build a presentation-site with Lovable or Google AI Studio; the source's favourite is Codex (chatgpt.com/codex, desktop app); Claude is equally strong — arguably prettier by default. The method
1

Teach it your style

  • Create a custom skill — a reusable set of instructions the agent follows every time.
  • Feed it your website, your brand PDF, your colours and fonts.
  • Ask for test presentations; critique them; iterate.
  • Expect to invest hours, perhaps days — like training a new colleague on your house standard.
Step 1: in Codex, use the "skill creator" to build a personalised presentation skill (Claude has the same concept). You show it your site, a brand PDF, ask for a test deck, then explain what you'd like different — repeatedly. It's genuinely time-consuming, and that's fine: you do it once and reuse it forever. Crucially: instruct it to produce HTML, a website — never a PPTX.
Step 1 · the payoff

Illustrations in your own style

Pair the presentation skill with an image-generation skill. Describe your palette — this ink blue, this aged-paper yellow — and your preferred style of illustration.

Every image in every future deck comes out matching. That consistency reads as personality.

a palette becomes a signature

Codex has an image-gen skill: illustrations generated in your chosen style, every time. (The source's own brand: ink blue and an aged-paper yellow — which this deck borrows as homage.) You discuss with the agent until you're happy with slides, illustrations, fonts and transitions. That closes Step 1.
The method
2

Give it a proper brief

"Turn this document into slides" is a recipe for mediocrity

Generic input produces generic output. What the AI needs is a script — detail, context, intent, slide by slide.

Step 2: the input. Don't dump a document and say "make this into slides", and don't say "make a presentation about X" — you'll get something averagely generic. What works is writing a presentation script — and again: a script for presenting, not merely for slides.
Step 2 · a workflow that works

You think out loud; the AI structures. Only when the script satisfies you does any slide get built.

The concrete routine: pick up the phone, start dictating, tell the AI it's a script for a presentation and ask it to organise your rambling into slide-by-slide ideas. Then work on that script in an ordinary text editor, chatting with the AI for more ideas and examples until satisfied. THEN take it to the agent.
The method
3

Generate & check

  • One instruction: "Use our skill to build this presentation from this script."
  • The agent works for 30–60+ minutes, depending on size.
  • It opens a browser and inspects its own slides, fixing what looks wrong.
  • What comes out is, essentially, a website.
Step 3 is almost anticlimactic: one sentence to the agent. It takes its time — half an hour, sometimes more — because it doesn't just write the code; it opens a browser and checks each slide looks right. This self-checking is what separates agents from chatbots.
Shipping it

Put it on the web in one step

  • Ask the agent to deploy via Cloudflare (a free skill) — you get a live address immediately, at no cost.
  • No files to carry, nothing to email. "When it's my turn, open this address."
  • Add a password if you like. Clicker works. Full screen works.
Cloudflare is an infrastructure provider that hosts sites; there's a skill for it. Ask the agent to put the site up and it hands back a URL on the spot, free. Your presentation now lives online: you walk into any room, borrow any laptop, open one address. Presenter remote and full-screen behave exactly as with PowerPoint.
Fair objection

"So far, this is just PowerPoint online."

True — with less faff, because AI is fluent in HTML. But the real power arrives when you remember your deck is now software.

Voice the audience's scepticism before they do. Concede it: up to this point we've built a nicer PowerPoint. Beat. "But your presentation is now a piece of software. And software can do things rectangles cannot." Everything from here is the demonstration.

A historical comparison

The first 30 years of electricity changed almost nothing

Factories unbolted the giant steam engine and bolted an electric motor into the same spot. Same layout, same energy, slightly cheaper. Essentially the same.

Now the answer to the quiz: roughly THIRTY years of flat productivity after electrification began. Announce how many in the room guessed right. Why so long? Factories kept their old architecture — one huge central engine, belts and shafts everywhere — and merely swapped the power source. Substitution, not rethinking.
The comparison, continued

Productivity jumped when factories were redesigned

  • Electricity's real advantage: power could be delivered anywhere — sockets moved, machines spread across the floor.
  • That freedom enabled the production line — and manufacturing was revolutionised.
  • We are at precisely that moment with AI.
The transformation came when people realised electricity's advantage wasn't cheaper rotation — it was that energy could be distributed anywhere in the building. Rearranged factories produced the assembly line. Then productivity exploded. Land the parallel firmly: using AI to make PowerPoints faster is bolting a motor where the steam engine stood.
The lesson

Don't use AI to do the old job the old way. Use it to rethink the job.

  • Not better dashboards — better decisions.
  • Not better spreadsheets — better models.
  • Not better PowerPoint — a succession of screens in service of your story.
Generalise beyond slides: many people use AI to do exactly what they already did, the same way, with the same tools — in the long run that's likely a waste of time. You don't actually want a PPTX; you want screens supporting your storytelling, your lesson, your information. Presentations were one of the first places the speaker felt this revolution first-hand — and we're at the very beginning.
